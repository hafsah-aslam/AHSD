\begin{abstract}
Learned network intrusion detection systems (NIDS) are adopted for their claimed ability to detect zero-day attacks, and operators must decide before deployment whether to trust that claim. The evidence offered is almost always leave-one-attack-class-out (LOACO) evaluation: one attack class is withheld from a randomly split benchmark and the detector is scored once by the area under the ROC curve (AUC). LOACO assumes that a withheld class stands for attacks that emerge later in time amid drifting benign traffic, but this assumption has not been tested. HoNE (Hold-out and Natural-novelty Evaluation) is a pre-registered, leakage-controlled paired methodology that scores the same detectors, data, backbones and seeds under LOACO and under natural novelty, where attack classes first appear after the training period, and measures their agreement, seed variability and detection at an operating point. On four NetFlow-v3 datasets and Lycos2017, 12 detectors on two recurrent backbones, five seeds and four rolling cut-offs (210 runs), LOACO predicts natural novelty sometimes, but not reliably. It cannot be run on day-scheduled captures; across 12 paired comparisons it keeps the leading family in all 12 but the best detector in only 1 (bootstrap agreement 0.17 on average), rankings agree only moderately (median Spearman correlation 0.55), and 75\% of detector AUCs are lower under natural novelty. Natural novelty raises seed-to-seed variation up to 17-fold, and family AUCs up to 0.75 catch at most 18\% of new attacks at a 1\% false-alarm rate, even with an oracle threshold. We turn each failure into one of eight acceptance checks an operator can require.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Network operators increasingly deploy machine-learning network intrusion detection systems (NIDS) because signature rules cannot cover attacks that did not exist when the rules were written. Consider an operator choosing between two learned detectors for an enterprise or IoT network. Both report high zero-day scores on public benchmarks; the operator deploys the better one, and months later a new attack class appears amid benign traffic that has changed since training. Whether the detector catches it depends on whether the benchmark evidence predicted exactly this situation.

The stakes are operational. An overstated detector misses the attacks it was bought to catch, and one whose performance varies between training runs gives no stable basis for alert thresholds or for staffing alert triage. Both surface only after deployment, so the validity of pre-deployment evidence is a network-management question, not only a machine-learning one.

The standard evidence for zero-day ability is \emph{leave-one-attack-class-out} (LOACO) evaluation: one attack class is withheld from training on a benchmark split at random, and the detector must flag it at test time~\cite{wilkie2026}. It is attractive because it runs on any labeled dataset and isolates a single unseen class, and it underlies the zero-day studies we review (Sec.~\ref{sec:related}).

LOACO, however, rests on assumptions that naturally emerging attacks do not satisfy. Random splits let near-identical flows and whole attack episodes appear in both training and test sets~\cite{arp2022}. LOACO keeps the benign traffic of the test period in training, so the withheld class is the only thing that changes, whereas a real new attack arrives \emph{later in time} alongside drifting benign traffic, which we call \emph{natural temporal novelty}. Results usually come from one run, are summarized by AUC, and often score novelty with confidence measures designed for multi-class image recognition~\cite{hendrycks2017,liu2020energy}. Such benchmark scores are known to overstate field performance~\cite{sommer2010,apruzzese2023sok}, yet whether LOACO conclusions transfer to natural novelty, a property we call \emph{protocol validity}, has not been measured: recent studies combine time-ordered splits with held-out attack families~\cite{nayak2026,alhalangy2026} or compare several protocols~\cite{mesca2026,raj2026}, but to evaluate a proposed detector rather than to test the protocol itself.

This paper therefore asks one question: \emph{can hold-out zero-day evidence predict natural novelty?} It has two parts. First, does LOACO evidence \emph{agree} with natural novelty: can it be produced on time-ordered captures, and does it select the same detectors, rankings and AUC levels? Second, what does hold-out evidence \emph{hide}, in run-to-run variability and in detection at the false-alarm rate an operator can absorb?

We answer it with \textbf{HoNE} (\textbf{Ho}ld-out and \textbf{N}atural-novelty \textbf{E}valuation), a paired methodology that treats the evaluation protocol, not the detector, as the object of study. HoNE builds leakage-controlled splits from flow timestamps and scores every detector under both protocols on the same data, backbones and five seeds. It then measures agreement (rank correlation, change of best detector, AUC and TPR differences), seed variability and operating-point detection, and checks them with stress and validity tests: purged-block LOACO, rolling training cut-offs, in-distribution temporal shift, cross-network transfer and a second backbone. All hypotheses were hashed before each experiment, and failed ones are reported (Sec.~\ref{sec:neg}).

Applied to five public datasets and 12 detectors, HoNE shows that \emph{conventional hold-out evaluation is not a reliable proxy for natural attack emergence}. Temporal LOACO cannot be run on any day-scheduled capture (Sec.~\ref{sec:c1}). Where both protocols run, the answer is \emph{sometimes, but not reliably}: LOACO's leading family survives in all 12 paired comparisons, but its best detector in only 1, rankings agree only moderately (median $\rho{=}0.55$), and 75\% of detector AUCs are lower under natural novelty (Sec.~\ref{sec:c2}). Natural novelty exposes instability that LOACO hides: seed variation rises up to 17$\times$ (2.6$\times$ on the second backbone; Sec.~\ref{sec:c3}). Neither inverted confidence scores nor temporal shift alone explains the disagreement (Secs.~\ref{sec:c4}--\ref{sec:c5}), and the gap matters at the operating point: family AUCs up to 0.75 catch at most 18\% of new attacks at a 1\% false-alarm rate, even with an oracle threshold (Sec.~\ref{sec:c6}).

These findings define what credible zero-day evidence must contain: each exposes a failure mode, and each failure mode yields a check an operator can require. Because the best detector changes with the protocol, a LOACO-only result cannot select a detector, so natural-novelty results are required wherever timestamps allow. Because seed variation hides under LOACO, a single run can misrank detectors, so five or more seeds with per-class spread are required. Because AUC hides missed attacks, detection at a fixed false-positive rate is required. The resulting \emph{HoNE acceptance protocol} has eight such checks (Sec.~\ref{sec:disc}), each cheap: one of our runs takes about three CPU-minutes.

Our contributions are as follows:
\begin{itemize}
\item We introduce HoNE, a paired, pre-registered methodology that tests whether hold-out zero-day evidence holds under natural novelty, on the same detectors, data, backbones and seeds (Sec.~\ref{sec:method}).
\item We show that it does not: LOACO is infeasible on day-scheduled captures, changes the best detector in 11 of 12 comparisons, hides up to 17$\times$ more seed variation, and its AUCs overstate detection at 1\% FPR (Sec.~\ref{sec:eval}).
\item We derive an eight-check acceptance protocol that operators can require before trusting a zero-day claim (Sec.~\ref{sec:disc}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

We review six lines of work: evaluation pitfalls and datasets, zero-day and open-set detection, temporal and protocol-aware evaluation, confidence scores for unknown attacks, cross-domain generalization, and detector architectures and robustness. Each informs HoNE, but none tests whether hold-out evidence remains valid under natural novelty.

\textbf{Evaluation pitfalls and datasets.} Arp et~al.\ cataloged ten pitfalls in security ML, including data snooping and inappropriate baselines~\cite{arp2022}; Apruzzese et~al.\ showed that ML-NIDS evaluations rarely reflect operational assumptions~\cite{apruzzese2023sok}; dataset audits found flow-construction and labeling errors in CIC-IDS2017~\cite{engelen2021,rosay2021}; and Pinto et~al.\ reviewed how NIDS datasets are built~\cite{pinto2025}. The NetFlow standardization of Sarhan et~al.\ made datasets comparable~\cite{sarhan2021netflow,sarhan2022standard}, and its third version adds per-flow timestamps~\cite{luay2025}. These works diagnose \emph{how} evaluations go wrong, but they do not compare zero-day protocols or measure what the pitfalls do to detector rankings.

\textbf{Zero-day and open-set detection.} Zoppi et~al.\ found that unsupervised meta-learning ensembles detect simulated unknown attacks better than other algorithm families~\cite{zoppi2023}, and Sarhan et~al.\ linked low zero-day detection rates to distribution distance~\cite{sarhan2023zeroshot}. Recent detectors train with known attacks, via a contrastive loss (CLAD~\cite{wilkie2026}) or a transformer with energy scoring~\cite{jodayree2026}. These studies measure zero-day ability, but all of them use class hold-out, usually without reporting seed variability, so their conclusions inherit LOACO's assumptions.

\textbf{Temporal and protocol-aware evaluation.} Time-aware evaluation changed conclusions in malware detection~\cite{pendlebury2019} and intrusion detection under drift~\cite{andresini2021}, and multi-period benchmarks such as AnoShift~\cite{dragoi2022} and MAWIFlow~\cite{schraven2026} test detectors on traffic from later years. For zero-day detection, ThreatFormer-IDS~\cite{nayak2026} and Alhalangy and Eltayeb~\cite{alhalangy2026} combine time-ordered splits with held-out attack families for one proposed detector, Mesca et~al.\ compare random and attack-held-out protocols on industrial traffic~\cite{mesca2026}, and Raj et~al.\ report future-time and leave-one-attack-type-out tests~\cite{raj2026}. Chronological splits on day-scheduled CIC-IDS2017 degenerate~\cite{moczkodan2026}, capture-day assembly~\cite{youssef2026} and temporal splits~\cite{abdessamad2026} change detector rankings, and timestamps leak the attack schedule~\cite{sakr2026}. These studies vary the protocol to test a detector; HoNE holds the detectors fixed and varies the protocol.

\textbf{Confidence scores for unknown attacks.} Confidence-based out-of-distribution scores are weaker on binary than on multi-class NIDS models~\cite{corsini2023}, closed-set deep classifiers assign unknown attacks to known classes, which motivated distance-based open-set detection in multi-class NIDS~\cite{zhang2021}, softmax entropy and energy trailed uncertainty-aware models~\cite{talpini2024}, and deep open-set recognition has been benchmarked for NIDS at NOMS~\cite{baye2023}. In contrast to Mesca et~al., who sign-flip ensemble members whose attack probability falls below chance~\cite{mesca2026}, we find the attack probability valid and MSP and energy inverted, and give a short derivation of why.

\textbf{Cross-domain generalization.} Layeghy and Portmann~\cite{layeghy2023xdomain}, DI-NIDS~\cite{layeghy2023dinids}, Cantone et~al.~\cite{cantone2024} and recent work on flow-based IoT detection~\cite{martinez2026} show that detectors degrade sharply on networks other than the one they were trained on. This line addresses shift \emph{between} networks, whereas an operator's first concern is shift \emph{over time within} the network being protected.

\textbf{Detector architectures and robustness.} Other work improves the detector itself rather than its evaluation: autoencoder ensembles~\cite{mirsky2018}, deep sequence and transformer models for flows~\cite{fernandez2026,manocchio2024}, and energy-efficient spiking neural networks~\cite{wang2024snn,knapp2025}; it also studies robustness to adversarial evasion~\cite{liu2026netmasq}, federated training across sites~\cite{xu2026entente}, and concept drift during deployment~\cite{depaola2026,yang2021cade}. These advances propose or harden detectors and are evaluated with the standard protocols; HoNE is orthogonal, asking whether those protocols give valid evidence for any such detector.

\textbf{Research gap and positioning.} Taken together, these lines leave the protocol validity of zero-day evidence untested; HoNE tests it by scoring the same detectors, data and seeds under both protocols. Table~\ref{tab:prior} summarizes the difference.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}
HoNE takes public flow datasets with per-flow timestamps and a panel of detectors, and determines whether zero-day evidence obtained under class hold-out agrees with detector behavior under natural temporal novelty. The methodology proceeds from these inputs through four stages. Section~\ref{sec:data} describes the input data; Section~\ref{sec:s1} (Stage~1) removes leakage and produces clean windows; Section~\ref{sec:s2} (Stage~2) splits them under paired zero-day protocols; Section~\ref{sec:s3} (Stage~3) trains and scores the detector panel over several seeds; and Section~\ref{sec:prereg} (Stage~4) turns the scores into metrics, findings and an acceptance decision. Together, these stages specify the complete path from raw flow records to an evidence-based decision on a zero-day claim.

\subsection{Input data}
\label{sec:data}
HoNE takes public flow-level datasets with per-flow timestamps as input~\cite{sarhan2021netflow,luay2025}. D1--D4 are the NetFlow-v3 versions of CSE-CIC-IDS2018~\cite{sharafaldin2018}, UNSW-NB15~\cite{moustafa2015}, ToN-IoT~\cite{moustafa2021} and BoT-IoT~\cite{koroniotis2019}, which share 53 features~\cite{luay2025}; D5 is Lycos2017, a corrected re-extraction of CIC-IDS2017~\cite{sharafaldin2018} that fixes documented flow-construction and labeling errors~\cite{engelen2021,rosay2021}, processed in its own feature space (Table~\ref{tab:data}). The five datasets cover enterprise (D1, D2, D5) and IoT (D3, D4) networks and differ in how attacks are scheduled: D1, D3 and D5 run each attack class on its own capture days, whereas D2 interleaves attacks in time, which later decides which protocols can be run (Sec.~\ref{sec:c1}).
Every flow carries a binary label and an attack-class label~\cite{sarhan2021netflow,sarhan2022standard}. For D1--D4 the dataset maintainers re-extracted each testbed's packet captures into this common NetFlow schema with millisecond start and end timestamps~\cite{sarhan2021netflow}, which makes strictly time-ordered splits possible; for D5 we run the authors' labeling script unmodified and map its timestamps and labels to the same fields. All files come from the official sources, are registered with their SHA-256 and row count, and are audited for schema conformity, missing and infinite values, label consistency and timestamp range before use; they are never modified. D4, whose capture is dominated by attack traffic~\cite{koroniotis2019}, contains only 1,315 benign windows and serves only as a transfer target, and D1's 14 file labels are grouped into six attack families matching the attack scenarios of the original capture~\cite{sharafaldin2018}, so that a held-out class has no near-identical sibling in training.

\subsection{Stage 1: Leakage control}
\label{sec:s1}
Stage~1 turns raw flows into windows that cannot leak information between training and test sets. Addresses, ports and DNS query IDs are dropped, and TTL fields are dropped as a known UNSW-NB15 label-leakage artifact; flow timestamps are kept only for ordering and splitting. Infinite values are imputed with the training median, heavy-tailed counts are $\log(1{+}x)$-transformed, and features are standardized with training statistics, leaving 41 common features over D1--D3. Flows are then sorted by start time and grouped into 1-hour blocks (10-minute blocks for D5's purged split) \emph{before} windowing into $T{=}32$ flows with stride 16, so that no window crosses a block. A window is an attack if any flow is, and its class is the majority attack class. Windows are hashed and exact duplicates removed globally, which eliminated 9.7\% of D3's windows.

Each build ends with fail-closed integrity checks on split disjointness, class ratios and held-out flows, all of which passed (Appendix~\ref{app:setup}). The output of Stage~1 is a set of leakage-controlled windows per dataset.

\subsection{Stage 2: Paired zero-day protocols}
\label{sec:s2}
Stage~2 derives every zero-day evaluation from two splits of the same windows. Both assign whole 1-hour blocks, so no window crosses a split, and both cut at cumulative shares of \emph{all} windows. The \emph{grouped\_random} split shuffles the blocks and fills training until it holds at least 60\% of the windows, then validation until training and validation hold at least 80\%; the remaining blocks form the test set. The \emph{temporal\_gap} split keeps time order: training takes the earliest blocks until it holds at least 60\% of all windows; the next 1-hour block that contains windows (empty hours are skipped) is discarded as a gap; validation takes the following blocks until training, gap and validation together hold at least 80\%; and the remaining blocks form the test period. The gap thus comes out of the nominal validation share, and because blocks are indivisible the realized shares are 60.2--62.3\% training, 0.05--4.4\% gap, 17.1--25.5\% validation and 10.4--19.8\% test across D1--D3 and D5. The evaluations form two levels. The \emph{primary paired comparison} scores every detector under (1)~LOACO and (2)~natural novelty. Four \emph{additional validity and stress evaluations} test that comparison: (3)~purged-block LOACO for the short D5 capture, (4)~rolling natural novelty, (5)~in-distribution AUC on the grouped and temporal splits, which isolates temporal shift without novelty, and (6)~zero-shot transfer of D1--D3 models to the other datasets.
\begin{itemize}
\item (1)~\emph{LOACO} runs on grouped\_random: one attack class is removed from training and tested on. A class is eligible if its training pool holds at least 200 windows (by majority label), and it is evaluated only if it also occurs in the test split.
\item (2)~\emph{Natural novelty} runs on temporal\_gap and scores the classes that occur in the test period but never in the training period. Windows containing any flow of these classes are removed from training and validation. On D1 this removes every attack window of the validation period, so, for all datasets, natural novelty takes its validation set from the training period instead (Amendment~3): models train on the first 80\% of the training period and select checkpoints on its last 20\%; the validation period is not used, and the test period is unchanged.
\item (3)~\emph{Purged blocks}, for short captures, assign 10-minute blocks 60/20/20 within each capture day and remove blocks adjacent to test or validation blocks, following purged cross-validation~\cite{lopezdeprado2018}; folds additionally require 50 test windows.
\item (4)~\emph{Rolling cut-points} (Amendment~7) re-apply the natural-novelty rule with the training period ending once it holds at least 30, 45, 60 or 75\% of each dataset's windows, followed by one discarded gap block, and testing on everything after; the cleaner is refitted on each cut's training rows, and each (dataset, cut, class) is one class--cut unit.
\end{itemize}
Training sets are balanced 1:1 (7,000 windows per class), and validation and test sets use a 5:1 benign-to-attack ratio. Protocols that cannot be run on a dataset are reported rather than skipped. The output of Stage~2 is one set of training, validation and test windows per dataset and protocol, shared by every detector.

\subsection{Stage 3: Detector panel and multi-seed runs}
\label{sec:s3}
Stage~3 trains and scores every detector on every split from Stage~2. HoNE groups 12 scores into five families by what they read from a model, adapted from the taxonomy of~\cite{yang2024ood} (Table~\ref{tab:det}). \emph{Confidence} scores read the classifier output; \emph{representation-distance} scores read hidden features~\cite{lee2018,sun2022knn}; \emph{benign-only} detectors never see attacks~\cite{liu2008,scholkopf2001}; the \emph{contrastive} detector CLAD~\cite{wilkie2026} is trained on benign and known attacks; and XGBoost~\cite{chen2016} on flattened windows is a supervised reference. Higher scores always mean more anomalous, and orientation was fixed before any run.

Confidence and representation scores come from AHSD (Adaptive Homeostatic Sequence Detector), a recurrent backbone we designed for this study and have not published elsewhere. AHSD was built to test whether anchoring a sequence model to normal traffic makes novel attacks stand out; those pre-registered hypotheses failed (Sec.~\ref{sec:neg}), so HoNE uses AHSD only as a backbone, in its fixed form, built around a benign reference $E$ that does not adapt. Each flow vector is embedded as $z_t=\mathrm{LayerNorm}(Wx_t)\in\mathbb{R}^{128}$, and a state $h_t$, initialized at $E$, tracks the input relative to it with per-step, per-channel gates:
\begin{equation}
h_t = h_{t-1} + \alpha_t\odot(z_t - E) - \beta_t\odot(h_{t-1}-E),
\end{equation}
with gates $[\alpha_t,\beta_t]=\sigma(W_g[z_t,h_{t-1}])$. $E$ is not learned: it is the mean embedding of benign training windows, tracked by an exponential moving average during training and set exactly afterwards. Stress $=\frac{1}{TD}\sum_{t,d}|h_{t,d}-E_d|$, and a two-layer head (linear--GELU--linear) reads $[h_T,\ \frac{1}{T}\sum_t h_t,\ \text{stress}]$ and outputs $P(\text{attack})$; its hidden layer is the representation used by Mahalanobis and $k$NN. Appendix~\ref{app:ahsd} gives the architecture diagram, pseudocode and training details. To test whether findings depend on this backbone, every confidence and representation score is recomputed on a second backbone, a one-layer GRU (hidden size 128) with the same embedding and head (without stress), trained identically (Amendment~7). XGBoost uses subsample and column sampling of 1 and is therefore deterministic; XGBoost-s uses 0.8 for both with the run's seed, so that the stability comparison is not made against a deterministic baseline.

Confidence scores need care in a binary detector. With logit margin $\Delta=f_{\text{attack}}-f_{\text{benign}}$, MSP $=\max(P(\text{attack}),\,1-P(\text{attack}))=\sigma(|\Delta|)$ measures how \emph{sure} the model is, not \emph{which} class it chose; temperature calibration rescales $\Delta$ and cannot change this ranking. A new attack that the classifier detects confidently has MSP close to 1, so the anomaly score $1-\text{MSP}$ is close to 0 and the attack reads as in-distribution; energy behaves the same way because it decreases as the largest logit grows. HoNE therefore reports $P(\text{attack})$ alongside MSP and energy; because it was added after the final pre-registered gate (marked $\dagger$), comparisons involving it are diagnostic rather than confirmatory.

Every detector--split configuration is run with five seeds. To keep the pipeline accountable, research questions, metrics and gates were written to a file and hashed before each experiment, and every result records that hash, the code commit and the dataset hashes~\cite{nosek2018}. Seven dated amendments are recorded, each hashed before the runs it governed, and none changed a recorded verdict: Amendments~2--4 followed failed gates, Amendment~6 turned the study into a descriptive benchmark after the fourth, and Amendment~7 added the GRU backbone, XGBoost-s, rolling cut-points and operating-point metrics. The output of Stage~3 is one anomaly score per window, detector, split and seed.

\subsection{Stage 4: Evidence and acceptance}
\label{sec:prereg}
Stage~4 turns the scores into evidence an operator can act on. For every detector and class it computes ROC AUC, PR-AUC and the true-positive rate at 1\% false-positive rate (TPR@1\%). TPR@1\% is read from the test ROC curve, i.e., with the threshold that gives exactly 1\% FPR on the test set's own benign windows. This threshold is an oracle: no deployed detector can know it in advance. TPR@1\% is therefore a descriptive \emph{upper bound}, not an estimate of deployable operating-point performance. A threshold fixed beforehand on earlier benign traffic either keeps test FPR within 1\%, and then detects at most this many attacks, or exceeds the false-alarm budget. Low TPR@1\% values thus show that detection is poor even under the most favorable threshold; high values do not show that it would be good in deployment. Results are reported as the mean over seeds with a Student-$t$ 95\% interval and the seed standard deviation, so that run-to-run variation is visible rather than averaged away. Per family, HoNE averages over detectors and classes and attaches a 95\% percentile bootstrap interval (2,000 resamples): each resample draws evaluation units (classes, or class--cut units for rolling cuts) with replacement and, independently, seeds with replacement, and applies the same draw to every family, so that family differences are paired. Because rolling units of one class share test windows, we also run a cluster bootstrap that resamples whole (dataset, class) clusters, with all of their units, together with seeds (19 clusters for the 45 rolling units), using the same pairing (Sec.~\ref{sec:c2}). HoNE then ranks the families for each protocol. 

\textbf{Protocol validity.} Let $\mathcal{D}$ be a detector panel evaluated on the same data, backbone and seeds under a proxy protocol $P$ (LOACO) and a target protocol $Q$ (natural novelty), and let $\varphi$ be a conclusion drawn from the scores: the best detector, the leading family, the order of two detectors, or whether a detector beats chance. The evidence from $P$ is \emph{valid for} $\varphi$ if $\varphi(P)=\varphi(Q)$. HoNE measures validity per conclusion type as the share of conclusions that survive, and summarizes the scores themselves by
\begin{gather}
\Delta\text{AUC}_d=\text{AUC}_d^{Q}-\text{AUC}_d^{P},\quad d\in\mathcal{D},\\
\rho=\operatorname{Spearman}\big(\{\text{AUC}^{P}_d\}_{d\in\mathcal{D}},\{\text{AUC}^{Q}_d\}_{d\in\mathcal{D}}\big),
\end{gather}
together with the natural-novelty rank and regret (AUC and TPR@1\% given up) of the detector LOACO would select. A paired bootstrap over classes and seeds (2,000 resamples) gives intervals and the probability that both protocols select the same detector. This analysis was added after the runs and is descriptive.

 Every evaluation is repeated with the final-epoch checkpoint to measure checkpoint sensitivity. All intervals are descriptive. Finally, these results are condensed into the HoNE acceptance protocol (Sec.~\ref{sec:disc}): eight checks an operator can require, which lead either to trusting a zero-day claim or to requesting the missing evidence.

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}
We use HoNE to answer six research questions, each of which tests one part of the central claim:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{RQ1:} Can conventional zero-day evidence be produced on time-ordered captures? (Sec.~\ref{sec:c1})
\item \textbf{RQ2:} Does conventional zero-day evidence agree with natural novelty? (Sec.~\ref{sec:c2})
\item \textbf{RQ3:} Does natural novelty expose instability that hold-out evidence hides? (Sec.~\ref{sec:c3})
\item \textbf{RQ4:} Are confidence-based novelty scores valid in binary detectors? (Sec.~\ref{sec:c4})
\item \textbf{RQ5:} Does temporal shift alone explain the disagreement? (Sec.~\ref{sec:c5})
\item \textbf{RQ6:} Does AUC overstate operational detection? (Sec.~\ref{sec:c6})
\end{itemize}
RQ1 and RQ2 test protocol validity directly; RQ3 and RQ6 test what hold-out evidence hides; RQ4 and RQ5 rule out alternative explanations.

All 210 final runs used one code commit and took 10.8\,h on a CPU-only container; training settings, seeds and compute are given in Appendix~\ref{app:setup}.

\subsection{RQ1: Can conventional zero-day evidence be produced on time-ordered captures?}
\label{sec:c1}
The first question is whether the standard protocol can even be run once leakage is controlled. Temporal LOACO yields no feasible folds on any of the three day-scheduled captures (D1, D3, D5); only D2, which interleaves attacks in time, supports it (Table~\ref{tab:feas}). In D1 and D3 each attack class occupies its own days, so a time-ordered split places a class wholly in training or wholly in test. On D5 the purged split kept 125 of 219 blocks, and only one class (dos\_hulk) met both thresholds. Natural novelty, by contrast, offers two to five evaluable classes on every day-scheduled capture, and moving the cut-point multiplies this evidence to 45 class--cut units over 19 unique classes, 15 per dataset (D2 has no test-only class at any cut, and D3 no benign validation windows at a 30\% cut).

\subsection{RQ2: Does conventional zero-day evidence agree with natural novelty?}
\label{sec:c2}
Where both protocols run (D1, D3, D5), we pair each detector's mean AUC under LOACO with its mean AUC under natural novelty, per dataset, backbone (AHSD: 12 detectors; GRU: its five scores plus the six backbone-free detectors) and natural-novelty view (fixed split; rolling cuts pooled), giving 12 paired comparisons (Fig.~\ref{fig:agree}).

\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.9\columnwidth]{fig_agree.pdf}
\captionof{figure}{LOACO vs.\ natural novelty: (a) AUC, (b) rank, (c) probability of the same best detector, (d) AUC change per detector.}

Fig.~\ref{fig:agree} summarizes the answer. \emph{Best detector:} LOACO's pick remains the natural-novelty winner in only 1 of 12 comparisons (D1: PCA under LOACO, Mahalanobis under natural novelty; D5: XGBoost, then a representation distance); its median natural-novelty rank is 4, and under resampling the two protocols select the same detector with probability 0.17 on average and at most 0.44. \emph{Ranking:} $\rho$ ranges from 0.26 to 0.92 (median 0.55), its interval includes zero in 4 of 12 comparisons, and 27\% of detector pairs reverse order. \emph{Level:} 75\% of the 138 detector--comparison cells score lower under natural novelty (median $\Delta$AUC $-0.057$, mean $|\Delta\text{AUC}|$ 0.128, up to 0.451), the mean $\Delta$AUC interval lies wholly below zero in 5 comparisons, and 9 cells cross from one side of chance to the other. \emph{Cost:} following LOACO gives up a median 0.023 AUC under natural novelty (at most 0.085) but up to 0.353 in TPR@1\%. The three D3 classes evaluated under both protocols show the same pattern class by class ($\rho$ 0.55--0.85; the best detector changes in 3 of 6 class--backbone pairs; mean AUC lower in all six). What LOACO does preserve is the leading family within a dataset (no change in 12 comparisons): its signal is coarse, not detector-level.

Margins between families are not preserved either (Fig.~\ref{fig:family}). On D3, benign-only and representation scores are close under LOACO ($+0.022$ [$+0.004$, $+0.047$]), whereas under natural novelty benign-only detectors lead by $+0.376$ [$+0.247$, $+0.500$] and representation scores fall to chance. CLAD never leads; it trails benign-only detectors on both D3 evaluations ($-0.123$ and $-0.075$). XGBoost exceeds the leading novelty family on LOACO D2 and natural novelty D3 and D5, and ties it on purged D5. The GRU selects the same leading family as AHSD in all seven evaluations, though absolute values differ (natural novelty D3: GRU representation 0.869, AHSD 0.522). Pooled over all classes of each protocol, the family winner does change (Fig.~\ref{fig:winners}): representation under LOACO, XGBoost under natural novelty, and benign-only detectors over the 45 rolling units (0.748 [0.699, 0.796] vs.\ representation 0.623 [0.566, 0.683]), where XGBoost trails by $-0.076$ [$-0.154$, $-0.009$] and scores 0.263 at a 45\% cut on D3. Resampling whole classes instead of units widens these intervals. XGBoost still trails benign-only detectors ($-0.076$ [$-0.137$, $-0.018$]), but the lead of benign-only detectors over representation scores becomes marginal ($+0.125$ [$+0.003$, $+0.253$]; across bootstrap seeds the lower bound lies between $-0.007$ and $+0.011$).

\obs{LOACO predicts natural novelty sometimes, but not reliably. Its leading family survives in 12 of 12 comparisons, but its best detector in only 1 of 12, its pairwise orderings in 73\%, and its AUC holds or rises in only 25\% of cells; picking by LOACO can cost up to 0.353 in TPR@1\%.}

\subsection{RQ3: Does natural novelty expose instability that hold-out evidence hides?}
\label{sec:c3}
A change of best detector could still be a matter of noise, so the third question is how much results vary across seeds, and whether LOACO reveals that variation. On the first backbone, every stochastic supervised score (the six backbone scores and CLAD) is less stable under natural novelty than under LOACO (Fig.~\ref{fig:sd}). The effect is smaller on the GRU: its representation scores become 2.6$\times$ less stable, its confidence scores 1.0--1.3$\times$. Seeding XGBoost (XGBoost-s) raises its seed standard deviation only to 0.014, so the instability belongs to learned representations under shift rather than to seeding as such, and its size depends on the backbone. The seeded benign-only detectors (Isolation Forest, autoencoder) stay below 0.018 seed standard deviation under natural novelty, below every supervised score's minimum of 0.074. On D3 natural novelty, $k$NN has a seed standard deviation of 0.33--0.40 AUC on four of five classes, so the same detector can rank first or last depending on the seed. Fig.~\ref{fig:d3} (Appendix~\ref{app:d3}) shows this on the three D3 classes evaluated under both protocols.

\obs{Natural novelty raises the seed variability of every stochastic supervised score, by up to 17$\times$ on AHSD and 2.6$\times$ for the GRU's representation scores.}

\subsection{RQ4: Are confidence-based novelty scores valid in binary detectors?}
\label{sec:c4}
The confidence family scored worst in Fig.~\ref{fig:family}, and Sec.~\ref{sec:method} (Stage~3) predicted why. MSP and energy fall below 0.5 wherever the classifier detects unseen attacks confidently (Fig.~\ref{fig:conf}). On LOACO D2, MSP is below 0.5 on every seed of all eight classes while $P(\text{attack})$ reaches 0.997--1.000 on the same models: the classifier detects the held-out attacks, and MSP reads that confidence as normality. The GRU repeats the pattern (LOACO D2: MSP 0.162 vs $P(\text{attack})$ 0.999; natural novelty D3: 0.245 vs 0.918). Across all 34 evaluation--backbone pairs, MSP exceeds $P(\text{attack})$ only in five rolling cuts where $P(\text{attack})$ is itself at or near chance (0.345--0.548), i.e., where the classifier misses the novel class. $P(\text{attack})$ falls below 0.5 only on individual classes (D3 LOACO ddos 0.35 and scanning 0.46; D5 portscan 0.16). Representation distance is valid on average, but on natural novelty D3 its interval [0.402, 0.647] includes 0.5.

\obs{In binary detectors, MSP and energy invert on both backbones; $P(\text{attack})$ and representation distance are valid on average.}

\subsection{RQ5: Does temporal shift alone explain the disagreement?}
\label{sec:c5}
Natural novelty mixes a new attack class with temporal shift, so this question isolates the shift itself by comparing in-distribution AUC on the grouped and temporal splits. Its effects are dataset-specific (Fig.~\ref{fig:temp}, Appendix~\ref{app:temp}). On D1 both models lose 0.27--0.29 AUC under the temporal split; on D2 neither changes by more than 0.014; and on D5 both improve. Only on D3 does the AHSD backbone degrade (0.664, seed s.d.\ 0.202) while XGBoost holds 0.978; the GRU degrades less (0.981$\rightarrow$0.857), so the size of this drop depends on the backbone. Across 108 zero-shot transfer cells (12 detectors $\times$ 9 source--target pairs among D1--D4), the median AUC is 0.500 and 67\% fall in [0.3, 0.6]. Checkpoint choice moves representation and CLAD family means by at most 0.073 and confidence scores by up to 0.205 (D5, 26 validation attack windows).

\obs{Temporal shift alone does not explain the disagreement: its effect depends on dataset and backbone, and on D5 in-distribution AUC rises under the time split while natural-novelty AUC falls. Zero-shot transfer is near chance.}


\subsection{RQ6: Does AUC overstate operational detection?}
\label{sec:c6}
An operator does not experience AUC but the share of attacks caught at a false-alarm rate the security team can absorb; base rates make this gap well known in principle~\cite{arp2022,apruzzese2023sok}, and some studies report recall at a fixed FPR~\cite{nayak2026,mesca2026,aslam2026bimodal}. Here we quantify it across protocols. AUC and detection disagree sharply (Fig.~\ref{fig:operating}). Under LOACO D2 the best families catch 87--99\% of held-out windows at 1\% false-positive rate (oracle upper bounds, Sec.~\ref{sec:prereg}). Under natural novelty D3, benign-only detectors reach an AUC of 0.90 but catch 12\%, and XGBoost 38\%. Over the 45 rolling class--cut units no family exceeds 18\% (XGBoost 0.18; benign-only 0.09 at AUC 0.75), and of the 333 detector--class points with AUC $\geq0.75$ under natural novelty, 184 catch fewer than 20\% of attack windows (median 0.15): novel attacks overlap the benign score tail.

\obs{Over the rolling cuts, family AUCs up to 0.75 catch at most 18\% of new attacks at a 1\% false-positive rate, even with an oracle threshold.}

\subsection{Pre-registered negative results}
\label{sec:neg}
Four confirmatory hypotheses were tested against frozen gates, and all four failed (Appendix~\ref{app:neg}); because every gate was fixed before its runs, none of these failures could be tuned away. AHSD's adaptive anchoring offered no advantage, which is why HoNE uses it only as a fixed backbone and why the study became descriptive (Amendment~6).

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:disc}
Our results show that hold-out evidence predicts natural novelty only partly. This section explains what HoNE contributes, why the two protocols disagree, what evidence operators should require, and the scope of our findings.

HoNE treats the evaluation protocol, rather than the detector, as the object of study. Under natural novelty, LOACO preserves a family-level signal, but not detector choice, ranking, absolute AUC, stability, or operating point. A LOACO result can therefore indicate which kind of detector to consider, but not which detector to deploy, how well it will score, or how many new attacks it will catch.

\textbf{Why protocols disagree.}
The protocols expose different forms of novelty. LOACO retains benign traffic from the test period during training, allowing supervised representations to separate a held-out class from familiar benign traffic. Natural novelty instead introduces attacks under a changing traffic distribution, weakening that reference and increasing detector and seed dependence. Benign-only methods avoid attack-dependent representations and are therefore more stable, but their accuracy depends on the protocol: they lead on D3 natural novelty yet trail by 0.17 AUC on D2 LOACO. Tree ensembles are hard to beat on tabular data~\cite{grinsztajn2022}, yet XGBoost falls to 0.263 AUC at one D3 cut-point, showing that strong supervised boundaries do not guarantee robustness to emerging classes.

\textbf{What the HoNE protocol requires.}
Each acceptance check in Table~\ref{tab:accept} addresses an observed failure mode: leakage requires leakage controls, protocol dependence requires natural-novelty evaluation, instability requires multiple seeds, inverted confidence scores require validated novelty scores, specialized detectors that lose to simple ones require benign-only and gradient-boosted baselines, weak operating-point performance requires fixed-FPR metrics, checkpoint sensitivity requires reporting it, and post-hoc claims require pre-registration or an amendment log. An operator should request the missing evidence before trusting a zero-day claim.

\textbf{Scope and future work.} Our findings establish protocol dependence for the evaluated detectors and datasets rather than all learned NIDS. The 45 rolling class--cut units are not independent, as units of one class share test windows; a class-level bootstrap widens the intervals and makes the rolling lead of benign-only over representation scores marginal; CLAD was reimplemented from its paper (0.972 on purged D5 against the reported 0.997~\cite{wilkie2026}); and natural novelty captures previously unseen attack \emph{behavior}, not new vulnerabilities with familiar behavior~\cite{jibunoh2026}. Future work will extend HoNE to additional backbone families, more natural-novelty classes, independent rolling evaluations, and operational traffic.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:concl}

This paper asked whether hold-out zero-day evidence predicts the new attacks an operator will face. We evaluated the same detectors, data and seeds under LOACO and natural novelty. The answer is: only partly. LOACO is infeasible on day-scheduled captures, and where feasible, the best detector is preserved under natural novelty in only 1 of 12 paired comparisons. Natural novelty also increases seed variability by up to 17$\times$, while AUCs of up to 0.75 correspond to at most 18\% of new attacks detected at a 1\% false-positive rate, even with an oracle threshold. Zero-day claims should therefore not rest on hold-out evidence alone. HoNE's eight acceptance checks specify the evidence operators should require before trusting such claims.
