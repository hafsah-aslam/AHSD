\begin{abstract}
Operators deploy learned network intrusion detection systems (NIDS) to catch attacks that did not exist at training time. Zero-day claims are typically supported by leave-one-attack-class-out (LOACO) evaluation, in which one attack class is withheld from a randomly split benchmark and the detector is scored once by the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). Whether this predicts behaviour on attacks that arrive later in time is untested. We introduce HoNE (Hold-out and Natural-novelty Evaluation), a pre-registered, leakage-controlled benchmark that scores the same detectors, data and seeds under LOACO and under natural novelty, where attack classes first appear after the training period. We evaluate four NetFlow-v3 datasets and Lycos2017 with 12 detectors on two recurrent backbones, five seeds and four rolling training cut-offs. Three findings stand out. First, the evaluation protocol decides which detector looks best: the winning detector family changes with protocol, dataset and cut-off. Second, a respectable AUC can hide missed attacks: AUCs of 0.53 to 0.75 catch at most 18\% of new attacks at a 1\% false-alarm rate. Third, one training run is not reliable evidence: natural novelty raises seed-to-seed variation by up to 17 times. HoNE turns these findings into eight pre-deployment checks for operators.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Network operators increasingly rely on machine-learning network intrusion detection systems (NIDS) because signature rules cannot cover attacks that did not exist when the rules were written. The value of such a detector lies almost entirely in its \emph{zero-day} behaviour: an attack it has already seen could equally be caught by a signature. Before a learned NIDS is trusted in production, therefore, someone must decide whether its zero-day claim is credible, and that decision usually rests on evidence supplied by the detector's developers.

The stakes of that decision are operational. A detector whose zero-day performance has been overstated misses the attacks it was bought to catch, while one whose performance varies from training run to training run gives the security team no stable basis for setting alert thresholds or deciding how many analysts are needed to check the alerts. Both failures surface only after deployment, when the cost of discovering them is highest. The quality of pre-deployment evidence is therefore a network-management question, not only a machine-learning one.

The evidence in current use has a remarkably uniform shape, and each of its parts rests on an assumption that is rarely tested. Zero-day ability is usually measured by withholding one or a few attack classes from training on a benchmark split at random, so that the detector must flag them at test time~\cite{wilkie2026}; we call the one-class-at-a-time form \emph{leave-one-attack-class-out} (LOACO). Random splits let near-identical flows and whole attack episodes appear in both training and test sets~\cite{arp2022}, and LOACO keeps the benign traffic of the test period in training, so the held-out class is the only thing that changes. Results usually come from one training run, and novelty is often scored with confidence measures such as maximum softmax probability~\cite{hendrycks2017} or energy~\cite{liu2020energy}, which were designed for multi-class image recognition. The security-ML literature has long warned that such benchmark scores overstate field performance~\cite{sommer2010,apruzzese2023sok}.

What has not been tested is whether LOACO evidence predicts the situation an operator actually faces: a new attack class that arrives \emph{later in time}, alongside drifting benign traffic, which we call \emph{natural temporal novelty}. Answering this requires scoring the same detectors, on the same data and seeds, under both protocols, with leakage controlled and run-to-run variability reported. Recent studies combine time-ordered splits with artificially held-out attack families~\cite{nayak2026,alhalangy2026} or compare random, held-out and leave-one-out protocols~\cite{mesca2026,raj2026}, typically to evaluate a proposed detector. Here the protocol itself is the object of comparison: we ask whether conventional class hold-out and naturally occurring temporal novelty reach the same verdict on the same detectors, data and seeds. It is not known in general whether a detector that wins under LOACO would still win, or even remain stable, under natural novelty.

To close this gap we introduce \textbf{HoNE} (\textbf{Ho}ld-out and \textbf{N}atural-novelty \textbf{E}valuation), an evaluation benchmark rather than a new detector. HoNE has four components (Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}): leakage-controlled splits built from flow timestamps; paired zero-day protocols that evaluate every detector under both LOACO and natural novelty; a panel of 12 detectors in five families, replicated on a second backbone and each reported with its seed variability and its detection rate at an operational false-positive rate; and an acceptance protocol that turns the benchmark's findings into checks an operator can require. The benchmark was pre-registered: hypotheses, metrics and gates were hashed before every experiment, all seven amendments are published, and the four confirmatory hypotheses that failed are reported (Sec.~\ref{sec:neg}).

Together, these components allow us to test whether zero-day evidence remains reliable when evaluation moves from artificial class hold-out to naturally occurring temporal novelty. Applying HoNE to five public datasets yields three main findings, each stated as an operator concern:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Changing the test changes which detector wins.} Rankings vary with the protocol, dataset and training cut-off across both tested networks. XGBoost matches or beats specialized zero-day detectors in four of seven fixed tests, but trails detectors trained only on normal traffic as the cut-off moves (Sec.~\ref{sec:c2}).

\item \textbf{A high AUC can still mean most new attacks are missed.} At a 1\% false-alarm rate, no detector type catches more than 18\% of new attacks, even when AUC reaches 0.75 (Sec.~\ref{sec:c6}).

\item \textbf{Retraining the same detector can give very different results.} Score variation across random seeds is up to 17 times larger than under LOACO (2.6 times on the second network), so a single run may misrepresent performance (Sec.~\ref{sec:c3}).
\end{itemize}

Three supporting results complete the picture. Time-ordered LOACO cannot be run on CIC-IDS2018, ToN-IoT or Lycos2017, because each attack occurs on its own days~\cite{sharafaldin2018,moczkodan2026} (Sec.~\ref{sec:c1}); in binary detectors, maximum-softmax and energy scores rank detected attacks as normal (Sec.~\ref{sec:c4}); and the effect of a time split depends on the dataset, while transfer to a new network is near chance (Sec.~\ref{sec:c5}).

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
Prior work on learned NIDS evaluation falls into five lines: evaluation pitfalls and datasets, zero-day and open-set detection, temporal and protocol-aware evaluation, confidence scores for unknown attacks, and cross-domain generalization. Each contributes a piece of what an operator needs, and each leaves a different piece missing.

\textbf{Evaluation pitfalls and datasets.} Arp et~al.\ catalogued ten pitfalls in security ML, including data snooping and inappropriate baselines~\cite{arp2022}; Apruzzese et~al.\ showed that ML-NIDS evaluations rarely reflect operational assumptions~\cite{apruzzese2023sok}; and dataset audits found flow-construction and labelling errors in CIC-IDS2017~\cite{engelen2021,rosay2021} and Pinto et~al.\ reviewed the tools and processes used to build NIDS datasets and their drawbacks~\cite{pinto2025}. The NetFlow standardization of Sarhan et~al.\ made datasets comparable~\cite{sarhan2021netflow,sarhan2022standard}, and its third version adds per-flow timestamps~\cite{luay2025}. These works diagnose \emph{how} evaluations go wrong, but they do not compare zero-day protocols or measure what the pitfalls do to detector rankings.

\textbf{Zero-day and open-set detection.} Zoppi et~al.\ found that unsupervised meta-learning ensembles detect simulated unknown attacks better than other algorithm families~\cite{zoppi2023}, and Sarhan et~al.\ linked low zero-day detection rates to distribution distance~\cite{sarhan2023zeroshot}. Recent detectors instead train with known attacks: CLAD learns benign structure with a contrastive loss~\cite{wilkie2026}, and Jodayree et~al.\ combine a transformer with energy scoring and extreme-value calibration~\cite{jodayree2026}. These studies measure zero-day ability, but all of them use class hold-out, usually without reporting seed variability, so their conclusions inherit LOACO's assumptions.

\textbf{Temporal and protocol-aware evaluation.} Time-aware evaluation changed conclusions in malware detection~\cite{pendlebury2019} and intrusion detection under drift~\cite{andresini2021}, and multi-period benchmarks such as AnoShift~\cite{dragoi2022} and MAWIFlow~\cite{schraven2026} test detectors on traffic from later years. For zero-day detection, ThreatFormer-IDS splits ToN-IoT in time and then removes chosen attack families from training, reporting five seeds and recall at 1\% FPR for a single model~\cite{nayak2026}; Alhalangy and Eltayeb combine chronological splits with leave-one-attack-family-out for one proposed detector~\cite{alhalangy2026}; Mesca et~al.\ compare random, attack-held-out and leave-one-out protocols on industrial traffic~\cite{mesca2026}; and Raj et~al.\ report future-time and leave-one-attack-type-out tests on RPL-IoT~\cite{raj2026}. Others show that chronological splits on day-scheduled CIC-IDS2017 degenerate~\cite{moczkodan2026}, that the way capture days are assembled reverses detector orderings~\cite{youssef2026}, that temporal splits on ToN-IoT change classifier rankings~\cite{abdessamad2026}, and that timestamp fields leak the attack schedule~\cite{sakr2026}. These studies vary the protocol to test a detector; HoNE holds the detectors fixed and varies the protocol, including seed variability and a moving training origin.

\textbf{Confidence scores for unknown attacks.} Confidence-based out-of-distribution scores are weaker on binary than on multi-class NIDS models~\cite{corsini2023}, softmax baselines scored below chance on held-out attacks in multi-class NIDS~\cite{zhang2021}, softmax entropy and energy trailed uncertainty-aware models~\cite{talpini2024}, and deep open-set recognition has been benchmarked for NIDS at NOMS~\cite{baye2023}. In contrast to Mesca et~al., who sign-flip ensemble members whose attack probability falls below chance~\cite{mesca2026}, we find the attack probability valid and MSP and energy inverted, and give a short derivation of why.

\textbf{Cross-domain generalization.} Layeghy and Portmann~\cite{layeghy2023xdomain}, DI-NIDS~\cite{layeghy2023dinids}, Cantone et~al.~\cite{cantone2024} and recent work on flow-based IoT detection~\cite{martinez2026} show that detectors degrade sharply on networks other than the one they were trained on. This line addresses shift \emph{between} networks, whereas an operator's first concern is shift \emph{over time within} the network being protected.

\textbf{Research gap and positioning.} Taken together, these lines leave one question unanswered: does the hold-out evidence used to support zero-day claims agree with how detectors behave under natural temporal novelty, on the same detectors, data and seeds? HoNE addresses it by treating the zero-day evaluation protocol itself as the object of comparison: it scores the same detectors, data and seeds under conventional class hold-out and under naturally occurring temporal novelty, with leakage controlled, seed variability reported and its hypotheses pre-registered.\ifappendix\ Appendix~\ref{app:related} extends this review to sequence, neuromorphic and drift-aware NIDS and positions HoNE against prior work in Table~\ref{tab:pos}.\fi

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}
HoNE takes public flow datasets with per-flow timestamps and a panel of detectors, and determines whether zero-day evidence obtained under class hold-out agrees with detector behaviour under natural temporal novelty. As summarized in Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}, the methodology proceeds from these inputs through four stages. Section~\ref{sec:data} describes the input data; Section~\ref{sec:s1} (Stage~1) removes leakage and produces clean windows; Section~\ref{sec:s2} (Stage~2) splits them under paired zero-day protocols; Section~\ref{sec:s3} (Stage~3) trains and scores the detector panel over several seeds; and Section~\ref{sec:prereg} (Stage~4) turns the scores into metrics, findings and an acceptance decision. Together, these stages specify the complete path from raw flow records to an evidence-based decision on a zero-day claim.

\subsection{Input data}
\label{sec:data}
\textbf{Input data.} HoNE takes public flow-level datasets with per-flow timestamps as input~\cite{sarhan2021netflow,luay2025}. D1--D4 are the NetFlow-v3 versions of CSE-CIC-IDS2018~\cite{sharafaldin2018}, UNSW-NB15~\cite{moustafa2015}, ToN-IoT~\cite{moustafa2021} and BoT-IoT~\cite{koroniotis2019}, which share 53 features~\cite{luay2025}; D5 is Lycos2017, a corrected re-extraction of CIC-IDS2017~\cite{sharafaldin2018} that fixes documented flow-construction and labelling errors~\cite{engelen2021,rosay2021}, processed in its own feature space (Table~\ref{tab:data}). Every flow carries a binary label and an attack-class label~\cite{sarhan2021netflow,sarhan2022standard}. For D1--D4 the dataset maintainers re-extracted each testbed's packet captures into this common NetFlow schema with millisecond start and end timestamps~\cite{sarhan2021netflow}, which makes strictly time-ordered splits possible; for D5 we run the authors' labelling script unmodified and map its timestamps and labels to the same fields. All files come from the official sources, are registered with their SHA-256 and row count, and are audited for schema conformity, missing and infinite values, label consistency and timestamp range before use; they are never modified. D4, whose capture is dominated by attack traffic~\cite{koroniotis2019}, contains only 1,315 benign windows and serves only as a transfer target, and D1's 14 file labels are grouped into six attack families matching the attack scenarios of the original capture~\cite{sharafaldin2018}, so that a held-out class has no near-identical sibling in training.

\subsection{Stage 1: Leakage control}
\label{sec:s1}
Stage~1 turns raw flows into windows that cannot leak information between training and test sets. Addresses, ports and DNS query IDs are dropped, and TTL fields are dropped as a known UNSW-NB15 label-leakage artifact; flow timestamps are kept only for ordering and splitting. Infinite values are imputed with the training median, heavy-tailed counts are $\log(1{+}x)$-transformed, and features are standardized with training statistics, leaving 41 common features over D1--D3. Flows are then sorted by start time and grouped into 1-hour blocks (10-minute blocks for D5's purged split) \emph{before} windowing into $T{=}32$ flows with stride 16, so that no window crosses a block. A window is an attack if any flow is, and its class is the majority attack class. Windows are hashed and exact duplicates removed globally, which removed 9.7\% of D3's windows.

Because a silent leakage bug would invalidate every later result, each build ends with fail-closed integrity checks covering hash- and block-level disjointness of splits, class ratios, the absence of held-out flows from LOACO training sets, and finite values. All passed, including 538 checks on the D1--D4 archives after Amendment~1 and 32 on the D5 purged-block build. The output of Stage~1 is a set of leakage-controlled windows per dataset.

\subsection{Stage 2: Paired zero-day protocols}
\label{sec:s2}
Stage~2 derives every zero-day evaluation from two splits of the same windows. \emph{grouped\_random} shuffles blocks 60/20/20 into training, validation and test sets, and \emph{temporal\_gap} orders them in time, with the first 60\% for training, one discarded gap block, then 20\% each for validation and test (Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}, panel~2). The evaluations form two levels. The \emph{primary paired comparison} scores every detector under (1)~LOACO and (2)~natural novelty. Four \emph{additional validity and stress evaluations} test that comparison: (3)~purged-block LOACO for the short D5 capture, (4)~rolling natural novelty, (5)~in-distribution AUC on the grouped and temporal splits, which isolates temporal shift without novelty, and (6)~zero-shot transfer of D1--D3 models to the other datasets.
\begin{itemize}
\item (1)~\emph{LOACO} runs on grouped\_random: one attack class is removed from training and tested on. A class is eligible if its training pool holds at least 200 windows (by majority label), and it is evaluated only if it also occurs in the test split.
\item (2)~\emph{Natural novelty} runs on temporal\_gap and scores the classes that occur in test but never in training; its validation set is the last 20\% of the training period.
\item (4)~\emph{Rolling cut-points} (Amendment~7) re-apply the natural-novelty rule with the training period ending at 30, 45, 60 and 75\% of each dataset's windows, one discarded gap block, and testing on everything after; the cleaner is refitted on each cut's training rows, and each (dataset, cut, class) is one class--cut unit.
\item (3)~\emph{Purged blocks}, for short captures, assign 10-minute blocks 60/20/20 within each capture day and remove blocks adjacent to test or validation blocks, following purged cross-validation~\cite{lopezdeprado2018}; folds additionally require 50 test windows.
\end{itemize}
Training sets are balanced 1:1 (7,000 windows per class), and validation and test sets use a 5:1 benign-to-attack ratio. Protocols that cannot be run on a dataset are reported rather than skipped. The output of Stage~2 is one set of training, validation and test windows per dataset and protocol, shared by every detector.

\subsection{Stage 3: Detector panel and multi-seed runs}
\label{sec:s3}
Stage~3 trains and scores every detector on every split from Stage~2. HoNE groups 12 scores into five families by what they read from a model, adapted from the taxonomy of~\cite{yang2024ood} (Table~\ref{tab:det}). \emph{Confidence} scores read the classifier output; \emph{representation-distance} scores read hidden features~\cite{lee2018,sun2022knn}; \emph{benign-only} detectors never see attacks~\cite{liu2008,scholkopf2001}; the \emph{contrastive} detector CLAD~\cite{wilkie2026} is trained on benign and known attacks; and XGBoost~\cite{chen2016} on flattened windows is a supervised reference. Higher scores always mean more anomalous, and orientation was fixed before any run.

Confidence and representation scores come from a recurrent backbone (AHSD) built around a fixed benign reference $E$. Each flow vector is embedded as $z_t=\mathrm{LayerNorm}(Wx_t)\in\mathbb{R}^{128}$, and a state $h_t$, initialised at $E$, tracks the input relative to it with per-step, per-channel gates:
\begin{equation}
h_t = h_{t-1} + \alpha_t\odot(z_t - E) - \beta_t\odot(h_{t-1}-E),
\end{equation}
with gates $[\alpha_t,\beta_t]=\sigma(W_g[z_t,h_{t-1}])$. $E$ is not learned: it is the mean embedding of benign training windows, tracked by an exponential moving average during training and set exactly afterwards. Stress $=\frac{1}{TD}\sum_{t,d}|h_{t,d}-E_d|$, and a two-layer head (linear--GELU--linear) reads $[h_T,\ \frac{1}{T}\sum_t h_t,\ \text{stress}]$ and outputs $P(\text{attack})$; its hidden layer is the representation used by Mahalanobis and $k$NN. To test whether findings depend on this backbone, every confidence and representation score is recomputed on a second backbone, a one-layer GRU (hidden size 128) with the same embedding and head (without stress), trained identically (Amendment~7). XGBoost uses subsample and column sampling of 1 and is therefore deterministic; XGBoost-s uses 0.8 for both with the run's seed, so that the stability comparison is not made against a deterministic baseline.

Confidence scores need care in a binary detector. With logit margin $\Delta=f_{\text{attack}}-f_{\text{benign}}$, MSP $=\max(P(\text{attack}),\,1-P(\text{attack}))=\sigma(|\Delta|)$ measures how \emph{sure} the model is, not \emph{which} class it chose; temperature calibration rescales $\Delta$ and cannot change this ranking. A new attack that the classifier detects confidently has MSP close to 1, so the anomaly score $1-\text{MSP}$ is close to 0 and the attack reads as in-distribution; energy behaves the same way because it decreases as the largest logit grows. HoNE therefore reports $P(\text{attack})$ alongside MSP and energy; because it was added after the final pre-registered gate (marked $\dagger$), comparisons involving it are diagnostic rather than confirmatory.

Every detector--split configuration is run with five seeds. To keep the pipeline accountable, research questions, metrics and gates were written to a file and hashed before each experiment, and every result records that hash, the code commit and the dataset hashes~\cite{nosek2018}. Seven dated amendments are recorded, each hashed before the runs it governed, and none changed a recorded verdict: Amendments~2--4 followed failed gates, Amendment~6 turned the study into a descriptive benchmark after the fourth, and Amendment~7 added the GRU backbone, XGBoost-s, rolling cut-points and operating-point metrics. The output of Stage~3 is one anomaly score per window, detector, split and seed.

\subsection{Stage 4: Evidence and acceptance}
\label{sec:prereg}
Stage~4 turns the scores into evidence an operator can act on. For every detector and class it computes ROC AUC, PR-AUC and the true-positive rate at 1\% false-positive rate (TPR@1\%), the quantity a security team experiences; the 1\% threshold is set on each test set's benign windows, so TPR@1\% is descriptive. Results are reported as the mean over seeds with a Student-$t$ 95\% interval and the seed standard deviation, so that run-to-run variation is visible rather than averaged away. Per family, HoNE averages over detectors and classes and attaches a paired bootstrap interval over classes and seeds (2,000 resamples), then ranks the families for each protocol; every evaluation is repeated with the final-epoch checkpoint to measure checkpoint sensitivity. All intervals are descriptive. Finally, these results are condensed into the HoNE acceptance protocol (Sec.~\ref{sec:disc}): eight checks an operator can require, which lead either to trusting a zero-day claim or to requesting the missing evidence.

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}
We use HoNE to answer six research questions, each of which decides one experiment:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{RQ1:} Can LOACO be run on time-ordered captures? (Sec.~\ref{sec:c1})
\item \textbf{RQ2:} Does the protocol change which detector wins? (Sec.~\ref{sec:c2})
\item \textbf{RQ3:} Is one training run enough evidence? (Sec.~\ref{sec:c3})
\item \textbf{RQ4:} Which novelty scores are valid for binary detectors? (Sec.~\ref{sec:c4})
\item \textbf{RQ5:} How do temporal and cross-network shifts affect detectors? (Sec.~\ref{sec:c5})
\item \textbf{RQ6:} Does AUC reflect detection at an operating point? (Sec.~\ref{sec:c6})
\end{itemize}
RQ2, RQ3 and RQ6 support the three main findings; RQ1, RQ4 and RQ5 support the remaining results.

\subsection{Evaluation setup}
Both backbones are trained with AdamW (learning rate $10^{-3}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$), cosine annealing, gradient clipping at 1.0, batch size 256 and 15 epochs; the best validation macro-F1 checkpoint is kept (CLAD: best validation AUROC). Seeds are 17, 23, 42, 101 and 202, and a seeding regression test runs before every experiment. All 210 final runs (Amendment~7) used one code commit and include the 160 runs of Amendment~6, whose results they reproduce exactly; reports are regenerated from stored result files only. Compute: a CPU-only cloud container (4 vCPUs, Intel Xeon at 2.10\,GHz, no GPU); the 210 runs took 10.8\,h (185\,s per run, both backbones and all detectors).

\subsection{RQ1: Is LOACO feasible on realistic captures?}
\label{sec:c1}
The first question is whether the standard protocol can even be run once leakage is controlled. Temporal LOACO yields no feasible folds on any of the three day-scheduled captures (D1, D3, D5); only D2, which interleaves attacks in time, supports it (Table~\ref{tab:feas}). In D1 and D3 each attack class occupies its own days, so a time-ordered split places a class wholly in training or wholly in test. On D5 the purged split kept 125 of 219 blocks, and only one class (dos\_hulk) met both thresholds. Natural novelty, by contrast, offers two to five evaluable classes on every day-scheduled capture, and moving the cut-point multiplies this evidence to 45 class--cut units over 19 unique classes, 15 per dataset (D2 has no test-only class at any cut, and D3 no benign validation windows at a 30\% cut).

\textbf{Answer to RQ1.} No: temporal LOACO cannot be run on any day-scheduled capture, whereas natural novelty provides 45 class--cut units.

\subsection{RQ2: Does the protocol change the winner?}
\label{sec:c2}
Given that both protocols can be run on some datasets, the next question is whether they agree. They do not (Table~\ref{tab:fam}). Representation-distance scores lead in five evaluations and benign-only detectors in two. The sharpest reversal is on D3: under LOACO, benign-only and representation scores are close ($+0.022$ [$+0.004$, $+0.047$]), whereas under natural novelty benign-only detectors lead by $+0.376$ [$+0.247$, $+0.500$] and representation scores fall to chance. CLAD never leads; it trails benign-only detectors on both D3 evaluations ($-0.123$ and $-0.075$). XGBoost exceeds the leading novelty family on LOACO D2 and natural novelty D3 and D5, and ties it on purged D5, while natural novelty D1 is hard for every family. The GRU backbone selects the same leading family as AHSD in all seven evaluations, so the reversal is not an artifact of one backbone; its absolute values can differ, however (natural novelty D3: GRU representation 0.869 against AHSD's 0.522, still below benign-only detectors at 0.898).

Pooling every class of each protocol shows the effect at a glance (Fig.~\ref{fig:winners}): representation scores win under LOACO, XGBoost under natural novelty, and benign-only detectors over the rolling cut-points. Over the 45 rolling class--cut units, benign-only detectors lead (0.748 [0.699, 0.796]) ahead of representation scores (0.623 [0.566, 0.683]; difference $+0.125$ [$+0.048$, $+0.204$]), and XGBoost trails the leading family by $-0.076$ [$-0.154$, $-0.009$]; at a 45\% cut on D3 it scores 0.263, below chance. The leader also moves with the dataset (representation on D1 and D5, benign-only on D3) and with the cut fraction (representation, benign-only, CLAD, benign-only from 30\% to 75\%).

\textbf{Answer to RQ2.} Yes: the leading detector family changes with protocol, dataset and cut-point, and identically on both backbones.

\subsection{RQ3: Is one training run enough evidence?}
\label{sec:c3}
A change of winner could still be a matter of noise, so the third question is how much results vary across seeds. On the first backbone, every stochastic supervised score (the six backbone scores and CLAD) is less stable under natural novelty than under LOACO (Table~\ref{tab:sd}). The effect is smaller on the GRU: its representation scores become 2.6$\times$ less stable, its confidence scores 1.0--1.3$\times$. Seeding XGBoost (XGBoost-s) raises its seed standard deviation only to 0.014, so the instability belongs to learned representations under shift rather than to seeding as such, and its size depends on the backbone. The seeded benign-only detectors (Isolation Forest, autoencoder) stay below 0.018 seed standard deviation under natural novelty, below every supervised score's minimum of 0.074; On D3 natural novelty, $k$NN has a seed standard deviation of 0.33--0.40 AUC on four of five classes, so the same detector can rank first or last depending on the seed. Fig.~\ref{fig:d3} shows this on the three D3 classes evaluated under both protocols.

\textbf{Answer to RQ3.} No: natural novelty raises the seed variability of every stochastic supervised score, by up to 17$\times$ on AHSD and 2.6$\times$ for the GRU's representation scores.

\subsection{RQ4: Which novelty scores are valid?}
\label{sec:c4}
The confidence family scored worst in Table~\ref{tab:fam}, and Sec.~\ref{sec:method} (Stage~3) predicted why. MSP and energy fall below 0.5 wherever the classifier detects unseen attacks confidently (Table~\ref{tab:conf}). On LOACO D2, MSP is below 0.5 on every seed of all eight classes while $P(\text{attack})$ reaches 0.997--1.000 on the same models: the classifier detects the held-out attacks, and MSP reads that confidence as normality. The GRU repeats the pattern (LOACO D2: MSP 0.162 vs $P(\text{attack})$ 0.999; natural novelty D3: 0.245 vs 0.918). Across all 34 evaluation--backbone pairs, MSP exceeds $P(\text{attack})$ only in five rolling cuts where $P(\text{attack})$ is itself at or near chance (0.345--0.548), i.e.\ where the classifier misses the novel class. $P(\text{attack})$ falls below 0.5 only on individual classes (D3 LOACO ddos 0.35 and scanning 0.46; D5 portscan 0.16). Representation distance is valid on average, but on natural novelty D3 its interval [0.402, 0.647] includes 0.5.

\textbf{Answer to RQ4.} The attack probability and representation distance are valid on average; maximum-softmax and energy invert in binary detectors on both backbones.

\subsection{RQ5: How do temporal and cross-network shifts behave?}
\label{sec:c5}
Natural novelty mixes a new attack class with temporal shift, so this question isolates the shift itself. Its effects are dataset-specific (Table~\ref{tab:temp}). On D1 both models lose 0.27--0.29 AUC under the temporal split; on D2 neither changes by more than 0.014; and on D5 both improve. Checkpoint choice moves representation and CLAD family means by at most 0.073 and confidence scores by up to 0.205 (D5, 26 validation attack windows).

\textbf{Answer to RQ5.} The effect of a time split depends on the dataset and the backbone, and zero-shot transfer to a new network is near chance.

\subsection{RQ6: Does AUC reflect detection at an operating point?}
\label{sec:c6}
An operator does not experience AUC but the share of attacks caught at a false-alarm rate the security team can absorb; base rates make this gap well known in principle~\cite{arp2022,apruzzese2023sok}, and some zero-day studies report recall at a fixed FPR~\cite{nayak2026,mesca2026}. Here we quantify it across protocols. The two disagree sharply (Fig.~\ref{fig:operating}). Under LOACO D2 the best families catch 87--99\% of held-out windows at 1\% false-positive rate. Under natural novelty D3, benign-only detectors reach an AUC of 0.90 but catch 12\%, and XGBoost 38\%. Over the 45 rolling class--cut units no family exceeds 18\% (XGBoost 0.18; benign-only 0.09 at AUC 0.75), and of the 333 detector--class points with AUC $\geq0.75$ under natural novelty, 184 catch fewer than 20\% of attack windows (median 0.15): novel attacks overlap the benign score tail.

\textbf{Answer to RQ6.} No: over the rolling cuts, family AUCs up to 0.75 catch at most 18\% of new attacks at a 1\% false-positive rate.

\subsection{Pre-registered negative results}
\label{sec:neg}
Before the benchmark became descriptive, four confirmatory hypotheses were tested against frozen gates, and all four failed: a spectral model of per-class detectability (pooled $\rho{=}0.326$, below 0.6); attack absorption by an adaptive equilibrium under supervised training (not reproduced; detectability peaks at a small adaptation rate); benign-drift re-anchoring (0 of 9 detectors met the improvement criterion); and benign-only superiority confined to natural novelty. The last gate's supervised family included MSP and energy, which Sec.~\ref{sec:c4} shows are invalid; the verdict stands as recorded. Reporting these failures is part of the evidence: the descriptive findings were recorded after the hypotheses failed, under hashed amendments, not selected to fit a narrative.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:disc}
\textbf{Interpretation.} The three main findings have a common cause: the protocols disagree because they change different things. LOACO keeps the benign traffic of the test period in training, so supervised representations place a held-out class far from benign traffic and distance scores excel (0.977 on D2). Under natural novelty the benign traffic itself shifts, and representations shaped by known attacks lose their reference point, which explains both the drop to chance and the seed dependence on D3; how far they fall depends on the backbone (GRU: 0.869). Benign-only detectors never depend on which attacks were trained on, which explains their stability, but not their accuracy: they lead on D3 natural novelty yet trail by 0.17 AUC on D2 LOACO. XGBoost's strength on fixed evaluations matches evidence that tree ensembles are hard to beat on tabular data~\cite{grinsztajn2022}; its collapse at some cut-points shows that a supervised boundary can also misplace a new class.

\label{sec:protocol}
\textbf{What works: the HoNE acceptance protocol.} Each check follows from a finding, through the failure mode it exposes, to the evidence an operator should require before trusting a zero-day claim:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Leakage controls stated.} Random splits share flows and attack episodes between training and test~\cite{arp2022}; require time-block grouping before windowing, window deduplication and purging for short captures.
\item \textbf{Natural novelty next to LOACO.} The winner changes with the protocol (Sec.~\ref{sec:c2}), so LOACO alone cannot select a detector; require natural-novelty results at several cut-points wherever timestamps allow, and a statement when a protocol is infeasible (Sec.~\ref{sec:c1}).
\item \textbf{Five or more seeds.} Natural novelty raises seed variation by up to 17$\times$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:c3}), so one run can misrank detectors; require per-class seed standard deviations, not only means.
\item \textbf{Valid novelty scores.} MSP and energy rank detected attacks as normal in binary detectors (Sec.~\ref{sec:c4}); require $P(\text{attack})$ or a representation distance validated under natural novelty.
\item \textbf{Two baselines.} XGBoost or the benign-only family matched or exceeded every other family in five of seven fixed evaluations, and benign-only detectors led over the rolling cut-points (Sec.~\ref{sec:c2}); require both a benign-only detector and a gradient-boosted tree.
\item \textbf{Operating-point metrics.} AUCs up to 0.75 caught at most 18\% of new attacks at 1\% FPR (Sec.~\ref{sec:c6}); require TPR at the false-positive rate the security team can absorb.
\item \textbf{Checkpoint sensitivity.} Checkpoint choice moved confidence scores by up to 0.205 on D5's small validation set (Sec.~\ref{sec:c5}); require it when validation sets are small.
\item \textbf{Pre-registration or a full amendment log}, including failed hypotheses (Sec.~\ref{sec:neg}), so that reported findings can be told apart from selected ones.
\end{enumerate}
If a check is unmet, the operator should request the missing evidence; the checks cost little, as each of our 210 final runs took about three minutes on four CPU cores.

\textbf{Remaining limitations.} Both backbones are recurrent, so attention-based models may behave differently, and the size of the seed and temporal effects already differs between them. HoNE's findings therefore describe the evaluated detector families under this flow representation and these protocols, not learned NIDS in general; what they establish is that evaluation conclusions depend on the protocol. D1 and D5 contribute two natural-novelty classes each in the primary split; the rolling cuts add 45 class--cut units, but units of one class at several cuts share test windows and are not independent. The 1-hour grouping, any-attack labelling, 200-window threshold and 5:1 test ratio were fixed in advance but are not the only reasonable choices; $P(\text{attack})$ was added post hoc; and CLAD was reimplemented from its paper (0.972 on purged D5 against the 0.997 its authors report on a random split~\cite{wilkie2026}). Natural novelty is novel \emph{behaviour}; many real zero-days exploit new vulnerabilities with familiar behaviour~\cite{jibunoh2026}, which no flow benchmark captures. Finally, all datasets come from testbeds, and natural novelty follows the capture schedule, which is closer to deployment than LOACO but is not live traffic.

\textbf{Broader implications.} For network management, the acceptance protocol can be written into procurement: a vendor can be asked to report results on HoNE's released splits, under both protocols and over several seeds.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:concl}
Operators must decide whether to trust learned NIDS on attacks that did not exist at training time, yet the evidence offered for such claims is usually a single LOACO run on a randomly split benchmark. We introduced HoNE, a pre-registered, leakage-controlled benchmark that scores every detector under both LOACO and natural temporal novelty, and applied it to five datasets, 12 detectors on two backbones and five seeds. Three findings stand out: the evaluation protocol decides which detector looks best; AUCs up to 0.75 meant at most 18\% of new attacks caught at a 1\% false-positive rate; and natural novelty raised seed variability by up to 17$\times$. The acceptance protocol turns these findings into eight checks operators can require. We release HoNE's splits, amendments and code so that any detector can be evaluated under both protocols.
