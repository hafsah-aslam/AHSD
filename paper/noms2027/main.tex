\begin{abstract}
Operators deploy learned network intrusion detection systems (NIDS) to catch attacks absent from their training data, yet detectors are validated by holding one attack class out of a randomly split benchmark (LOACO). We ask whether this laboratory protocol predicts behaviour when a new attack class appears later in time, alongside drifting benign traffic. We build a pre-registered, leakage-controlled benchmark on four NetFlow-v3 datasets and LYCOS-IDS2017, with time-block splits and deduplicated flow windows, and evaluate 12 detectors from five families over five seeds. Three findings matter for operators. First, LOACO is often infeasible on time-ordered captures: temporal splits yield zero hold-out folds on two of three day-scheduled datasets, while natural temporal novelty remains measurable. Second, the protocols disagree: the best detector family changes with dataset and protocol, and a plain XGBoost classifier matches or beats the best novelty detector in four of seven evaluations. Third, natural novelty raises the seed variability of every supervised score 1.7--17-fold, and max-softmax and energy scores fall below chance because confidently detected attacks read as normal. We derive an evaluation checklist for detector assurance and release all splits, code and pre-registration records.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{A}{network} operator who deploys a learned network intrusion detection system (NIDS) is buying protection against attacks that are not in its training data. Before trusting the detector, the operator needs evidence that it raises an alarm when a new attack class first appears in the network. This is a security-management decision with operational cost: a detector that misses novel attacks creates false assurance, and one whose behaviour changes from one retraining to the next cannot be operated predictably.

\emph{The problem.} The evidence operators receive comes from a laboratory protocol, leave-one-attack-class-out (LOACO): one attack class is removed from a randomly split benchmark and the detector must flag it at test time. Recent zero-day NIDS work, including contrastive training~\cite{wilkie2026} and transformer-based open-set detection~\cite{jodayree2026}, reports results under this protocol. LOACO, however, differs from deployment in three ways. It removes a class artificially while keeping benign traffic from the same hours as the test set; it ignores time, although new attacks arrive after training together with new hosts, tools and benign drift; and it is usually reported for one training run. Security-ML research has repeatedly shown that such gaps inflate results~\cite{sommer2010,arp2022,pendlebury2019,apruzzese2023}.

\emph{The gaps.} We identify three gaps in current NIDS evaluation. (G-1) No study compares class hold-out with \emph{natural temporal novelty}, in which attack classes appear only after the training period, on the same detectors and data, so it is unknown whether LOACO results predict deployment. (G-2) Many NIDS papers score novelty with classifier-confidence scores designed for multi-class image recognition, such as maximum softmax probability (MSP)~\cite{hendrycks2017} and energy~\cite{liu2020energy}, without asking whether they are valid for a two-class detector. (G-3) Seed variability is rarely reported per class and hypotheses are rarely pre-registered~\cite{nosek2018}, so selective reporting cannot be ruled out.

\emph{Research questions.} We ask: \textbf{RQ1} Is LOACO feasible on realistic, time-ordered captures, and is natural novelty an available alternative? \textbf{RQ2} Do LOACO and natural novelty agree on which detector family is best, and how stable are the scores across training seeds? \textbf{RQ3} Which novelty scores are valid for binary NIDS, and how do temporal and cross-dataset shifts affect supervised detectors?

\emph{Approach and novelty.} We build a pre-registered, leakage-controlled benchmark on five public datasets: four NetFlow-v3 datasets~\cite{luay2025} and LYCOS-IDS2017~\cite{rosay2021}. Flows are grouped into time blocks before windowing, windows are deduplicated by hash, and every split is checked fail-closed. We evaluate 12 detectors in five families (classifier-confidence, representation-distance, benign-only, contrastive and a supervised XGBoost reference) under LOACO, natural temporal novelty, a purged time-block hold-out, in-distribution splits and zero-shot cross-dataset transfer, each over five seeds. To our knowledge, this is the first study that evaluates the same NIDS detectors under both class hold-out and natural temporal novelty, the first to report per-class seed variability across detector families for zero-day NIDS, and the first pre-registered NIDS benchmark that reports its failed hypotheses. Every number in this paper comes from one code commit and is regenerated from stored result files.

\emph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{C1 Protocol feasibility (RQ1).} On day-scheduled captures, temporal LOACO yields zero feasible folds on CIC-IDS2018 and ToN-IoT and one on LYCOS-IDS2017 under a purged block split, while natural novelty provides 2--5 evaluable classes per dataset.
\item \textbf{C2 No family dominates (RQ2).} The best family changes with dataset and protocol, and XGBoost matches or beats the leading novelty family in four of seven evaluations.
\item \textbf{C3 Hidden instability (RQ2).} Natural novelty raises the seed standard deviation of every supervised score 1.7--17$\times$ over LOACO, while seeded benign-only detectors stay stable.
\item \textbf{C4 Score validity (RQ3).} MSP and energy fall below chance in binary NIDS; the classifier's attack probability does not on average.
\item \textbf{C5 Generalization (RQ3).} Temporal-split effects are dataset-specific, and 67\% of 108 zero-shot cross-dataset results fall between 0.3 and 0.6 AUC.
\item \textbf{C6 Negative results and release.} Four pre-registered hypotheses failed their frozen gates; we report them and release splits, code and amendments.
\end{itemize}
From these we derive a six-point checklist that operators can use to assure a detection service before deployment (Section~\ref{sec:disc}). \section{Background}\label{sec:bg}
\subsection{Flow-based NIDS and windowing}
Flow-based NIDS summarize each connection as a record of counters and timings exported by network monitoring infrastructure. Sarhan et al. converted several public captures into a common NetFlow feature set so that detectors can be compared across networks~\cite{sarhan2021netflow}; the third version (v3) adds temporal features and per-flow start and end timestamps~\cite{luay2025}, which make strictly time-ordered splits possible. Sequence detectors consume windows of $T$ consecutive flows. Windowing creates two leakage risks: overlapping windows can place near-identical samples in training and test, and flows from one attack episode can straddle the split. Grouping flows into contiguous time blocks before windowing and deduplicating windows by hash remove both.

\subsection{Zero-day evaluation protocols}
Three protocols are compared in this paper (Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}, stage 4). \emph{LOACO} removes one attack class from the training pool; the test set holds benign windows plus that class, and splits are random, here grouped by 1-hour blocks. \emph{Natural temporal novelty} splits flows in time; attack classes that occur only after the training period are unseen by construction, and benign traffic drifts as it would in operation. \emph{Purged block LOACO}, for short captures, assigns short time blocks to splits within each day and drops blocks adjacent to test or validation blocks, following purged cross-validation for time series~\cite{lopezdeprado2018}. LOACO asks whether a detector generalizes to a missing class under the training distribution; natural novelty asks the operator's question, under real temporal shift.



\subsection{Novelty scores and the binary pitfall}
Out-of-distribution (OOD) detection assigns each input a score that should be higher for inputs unlike the training data. \emph{Classifier-confidence} scores read the output layer: MSP scores $1-\max_k p_k$~\cite{hendrycks2017} and energy scores $-\log\sum_k e^{f_k}$ over logits $f_k$~\cite{liu2020energy}. \emph{Representation-distance} scores measure how far hidden features lie from benign training features, e.g., Mahalanobis~\cite{lee2018} or nearest-neighbour distance~\cite{sun2022knn}. \emph{Benign-only} detectors never see attacks~\cite{liu2008if,scholkopf2001,mirsky2018}. \emph{Contrastive} detectors such as CLAD~\cite{wilkie2026} learn an embedding from benign and known attacks. MSP and energy measure how sure a classifier is, not which class it chose. In a two-class NIDS, a new attack that is confidently classified as \emph{attack} receives a low MSP score and therefore reads as in-distribution: the expected outcome of successful detection becomes a missed novelty. This pitfall is rare in many-class image benchmarks but structural in binary detectors.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}
\emph{Evaluation pitfalls and dataset quality.} Sommer and Paxson argued that anomaly detection outside a closed world is harder than benchmark scores suggest~\cite{sommer2010}. Arp et al. catalogued ten pitfalls in security ML, including sampling bias and data snooping~\cite{arp2022}, and TESSERACT showed that ignoring time inflates malware-detection results~\cite{pendlebury2019}. For NIDS, Apruzzese et al. found that evaluations rarely reflect operational conditions~\cite{apruzzese2023}, and Hus\'ak et al. reported, from a network-management perspective, that ML intrusion detection research favours ML metrics over operational outcomes~\cite{husak2024}. Dataset audits found labelling and flow-construction errors in CIC-IDS2017/2018~\cite{engelen2021,liu2022errors}, corrected in LYCOS-IDS2017~\cite{rosay2021}; design smells in benchmark NIDS datasets that cause severe experimental bias~\cite{flood2024}; and metadata features that let models shortcut the task~\cite{dhooge2022}. These works diagnose problems; we measure how one of them, the choice of zero-day protocol, changes which detector an operator would deploy.

\emph{Zero-day and open-set NIDS.} A recent survey in this venue's journal concludes that ML intrusion detectors detect known attacks well but unknown, atypical and polymorphic attacks poorly~\cite{sabeel2024}. Sarhan et al. framed zero-day detection as zero-shot learning and linked low detection rates to large Wasserstein distance from known attacks~\cite{sarhan2023zeroday}. Zoppi et al. compared 47 algorithms on unknown attacks and found unsupervised meta-learners strongest~\cite{zoppi2023}. Wilkie et al. proposed CLAD, a contrastive loss trained on benign and known attacks~\cite{wilkie2026}; Jodayree et al. combined a transformer with energy scoring and extreme-value calibration, reporting an AUROC of 0.975 under leave-one-attack-out on IoMT traffic~\cite{jodayree2026}; Thi and Gurusamy detect open-set attacks with a model trained on benign traffic only~\cite{thi2026}; and Zhang et al. detect unknown attacks from inter-class relationships~\cite{zhang2026tifs}. Kitsune detects anomalies with autoencoders trained on benign traffic~\cite{mirsky2018}. All of these evaluate under class hold-out or in-distribution; none tests natural temporal novelty, and none questions whether energy-style scores suit binary detectors.

\emph{Drift and temporal evaluation of NIDS.} INSOMNIA applied time-aware evaluation to intrusion detection and found that accounting for drift is vital~\cite{andresini2021}. ReCDA~\cite{yang2024recda} and continual-learning NIDS~\cite{zhang2025infocom} adapt detectors to drifting traffic, HOIDS adapts an online NIDS~\cite{depaola2026}, CADE explains drifting samples~\cite{yang2021cade}, and adaptive attackers can evade ML traffic detectors without model access~\cite{liu2026netmasquerade}. These works adapt detectors to drift but do not ask which zero-day protocol an operator should trust.

\emph{Cross-domain generalization.} NetFlow NIDS are commonly evaluated in-distribution, e.g., the graph-based E-GraphSAGE~\cite{lo2022egraphsage}. Layeghy and Portmann~\cite{layeghy2023xdomain} and Cantone et al.~\cite{cantone2024} found that near-perfect within-dataset performance falls towards chance across datasets; recent IoT studies examine cross-domain strategies and deep-learning practice~\cite{martinez2026,fernandez2026}. These measure transfer between networks but not novelty within one network.

\subsection{Stage 1: Input datasets}
All five datasets are public, were downloaded from their official sources and are recorded in a manifest with SHA-256, row count and column list (Table~\ref{tab:datasets}). D1--D4 are the NetFlow-v3 releases of the University of Queensland~\cite{luay2025}, which re-extract each capture's packet traces into 53 NetFlow features with per-flow start and end timestamps in milliseconds, using a common feature set across datasets~\cite{sarhan2021netflow}.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{D1 CSE-CIC-IDS2018}~\cite{sharafaldin2018} was generated by the Canadian Institute for Cybersecurity on a cloud-hosted enterprise testbed with scripted benign users and attack machines; our copy spans 14~Feb--2~Mar 2018. Its 14 attack labels (brute force, botnet, DoS, DDoS, web attacks, infiltration) map onto six families. Known labelling errors in the original release~\cite{liu2022errors} motivate our family-level analysis.
\item \textbf{D2 UNSW-NB15}~\cite{moustafa2015} was created in the UNSW Canberra Cyber Range with a traffic generator that mixes real normal activity with nine synthetic attack families (e.g., exploits, fuzzers, reconnaissance, backdoors); it spans 22~Jan--18~Feb 2015.
\item \textbf{D3 ToN-IoT}~\cite{moustafa2021} comes from an IoT/IIoT testbed with edge, fog and cloud layers and nine attack types (scanning, DoS, DDoS, ransomware, backdoor, injection, cross-site scripting, password and man-in-the-middle); it spans 23--29~Apr 2019.
\item \textbf{D4 BoT-IoT}~\cite{koroniotis2019} simulates IoT devices under botnet attacks (DDoS, DoS, reconnaissance and data theft); it spans May--Jun 2018 but contains only 1,315 benign windows, so it serves only as a transfer target.
\item \textbf{D5 LYCOS-IDS2017}~\cite{rosay2021} re-processes the CIC-IDS2017 packet traces (five working days, 3--7~Jul 2017) with a corrected flow extractor and relabelling that fix documented flow-construction and labelling errors~\cite{engelen2021,liu2022errors}. We ran the authors' labelling script unmodified, kept its 73 own features and do not use D5 for transfer.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Stage 2: Leakage-controlled processing}
Each step addresses a known source of optimistic bias~\cite{arp2022,flood2024,dhooge2022}.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Audit and drop.} We record rows, types, missing values and class counts, then drop identifier columns (addresses, ports, DNS query ID), whose values identify hosts rather than behaviour~\cite{dhooge2022}, and minimum/maximum TTL, a known label-leakage artifact. Timestamps are kept only for ordering and splitting.
\item \emph{Clean with training statistics only.} Infinite values become missing and are imputed with the training median; non-negative heavy-tailed counts are transformed with $\log(1+x)$; features are standardized with training mean and deviation; zero-variance features and one of each pair with $|r|\geq0.999999$ are removed. For D1--D4 the intersection leaves 41 common features. No statistic is ever fitted on test or transfer-target data.
\item \emph{Sort and group in time.} Flows are sorted by start time and grouped into contiguous 1-hour blocks (10-minute blocks for D5's purged split), so that flows of one attack episode stay together.
\item \emph{Window and label.} Windows of $T{=}32$ consecutive flows with stride 16 are cut inside blocks and never cross a block boundary. A window is an attack if any flow is an attack, and its class is the majority attack class.
\item \emph{Deduplicate globally.} Every window is hashed over its raw features and exact duplicates are removed across the whole dataset, so that no window appears in both training and test (9.7\% of D3 and 15.8\% of D4 windows were duplicates).
\item \emph{Split and balance.} Blocks, never individual flows or windows, are assigned to splits (Section~\ref{sec:protocols}). Training sets are balanced 1:1 (7,000 benign and 7,000 attack windows); validation and test sets use a 5:1 benign-to-attack ratio, capped at 2,500 benign and 500 attack windows.
\item \emph{Fail-closed checks.} The pipeline stops if any check fails: row and block disjointness across splits, deduplication, balance, class composition and feature lists. 538 checks ran at preprocessing for D1--D4 and further checks for every derived split (e.g., 32 for D5's purged split); all passed.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Stage 3: Backbone and detectors}
\emph{Backbone.} All supervised scores are read from one recurrent backbone, the fixed-equilibrium variant of a homeostatic sequence detector. Each flow vector is embedded as $z_t=\mathrm{LayerNorm}(W x_t)\in\mathbb{R}^{128}$. A state $h_t$, initialised at a benign reference $E$, tracks the input through per-step, per-channel gates:
\begin{align*}
h_t &= h_{t-1} + \alpha_t\odot(z_t-E) - \beta_t\odot(h_{t-1}-E),\\
[\alpha_t,\beta_t] &= \sigma\big(W_g[z_t,h_{t-1}]\big).
\end{align*}
$E$ is not learned: it is the mean embedding of benign training windows, tracked by an exponential moving average during training and set exactly afterwards, and it stays fixed within a window. Homeostatic \emph{stress} is $\frac{1}{TD}\sum_{t,d}|h_{t,d}-E_d|$. A two-layer MLP head (linear--GELU--linear) reads $[h_T,\ \frac{1}{T}\sum_t h_t,\ \text{stress}]$ and outputs two logits; its hidden layer is the penultimate representation. We chose this backbone because it exposes a representation-distance score inside the model.

\emph{Detectors.} Twelve detectors in five families score each test window; higher scores always mean more anomalous, and orientation was fixed before any run.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Classifier-confidence} (from the backbone's logits): MSP, energy, and P(attack), the softmax attack probability, which was added after the last confirmatory gate and is marked \dg.
\item \emph{Representation-distance} (from the backbone's states): stress; Mahalanobis distance to a Ledoit--Wolf Gaussian fitted on benign penultimate features~\cite{lee2018}; and distance to the 10th benign nearest neighbour of $\ell_2$-normalised penultimate features~\cite{sun2022knn}.
\item \emph{Benign-only} (fitted on benign training windows, never seeing attacks): Isolation Forest with 200 trees~\cite{liu2008if}, one-class SVM with RBF kernel and $\nu{=}0.1$~\cite{scholkopf2001}, PCA reconstruction error at 95\% retained variance, and a fully connected autoencoder.
\item \emph{Contrastive}: CLAD~\cite{wilkie2026}, trained on benign and known attacks and implemented from its paper, with the details it leaves open fixed before training.
\item \emph{Supervised reference}: XGBoost~\cite{chen2016xgb} on flattened windows (300 trees, depth 6, learning rate 0.1), scored by its attack probability.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Stage 4: Evaluation protocols}\label{sec:protocols}
\emph{grouped\_random} shuffles blocks into 60/20/20 train/validation/test. \emph{temporal\_gap} orders blocks in time, takes the first 60\% for training, discards one gap block, and uses the next 20\% for validation and the last 20\% for test. On these splits we run five settings.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{LOACO} (grouped\_random): a class is held out if it has at least 200 training-pool windows (by majority label) and at least one test window; training and validation exclude every window containing any flow of that class. Every resulting fold has at least 81 held-out test windows.
\item \emph{Natural temporal novelty} (temporal\_gap): classes that occur in the test period but not in the training period are scored as unseen against test benign windows. Validation is the last 20\% of the training period (train-tail validation), because on D1 every temporal validation window contains a natural-novelty flow.
\item \emph{Purged block LOACO} (D5): 10-minute blocks are split 60/20/20 within each capture day; training and validation blocks adjacent to a test block, and training blocks adjacent to a validation block, are removed~\cite{lopezdeprado2018}. Here a held-out class also needs at least 50 test windows.
\item \emph{In-distribution}: binary detection on the test split of both split schemes.
\item \emph{Zero-shot transfer}: models trained on D1--D3 are applied to every other NetFlow-v3 dataset, including D4; every detector reference ($E$, benign-only fits, Mahalanobis, kNN and CLAD references) comes from the source network, and nothing is fitted on the target.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Pre-registration and provenance}\label{sec:prereg}
Research questions, metrics and acceptance gates were written to a file and hashed before each experiment; every result file records that hash, the code commit and the dataset hashes. Six dated, hashed amendments changed the plan, each before the runs it governs: two because data counts made a frozen rule infeasible (an empty validation split; zero LOACO folds on D5), three that set a new direction after a gate returned STOP, and one that added P(attack) and the family split after the last gate. Four confirmatory gates were evaluated exactly as frozen. The final benchmark reported here is descriptive: it applies no thresholds and reports intervals rather than significance tests.

\section{Experimental Setup}\label{sec:setup}
\emph{Training.} The backbone is trained with AdamW (learning rate $10^{-3}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$), cosine annealing, gradient clipping at 1.0, batch size 256 and 15 epochs, with no per-detector tuning. The checkpoint with the best validation macro-F1 is primary (for CLAD, the best validation AUROC). The final-epoch state of the same training run is also kept and scored as a sensitivity analysis. XGBoost uses subsampling of 1, which makes it deterministic.

\emph{Evaluations.} LOACO covers D1 families, D2 and D3 (6, 8 and 6 folds); natural novelty covers D1, D3 and D5 (2, 5 and 2 classes); purged LOACO covers D5 (1 fold); in-distribution detection covers D1, D2, D3 and D5 under both splits; and transfer covers 9 source--target pairs.

\emph{Metrics and statistics.} Seeds are 17, 23, 42, 101 and 202, and a seeding regression test runs before every experiment. For each detector and class we report ROC AUC as the mean over seeds with a Student-$t$ 95\% interval ($n{=}5$, $t{=}2.776$), the seed standard deviation and the fraction of seeds below 0.5. Family means average the family's detectors and the evaluation's classes, with a 95\% bootstrap interval that resamples classes and seeds independently (2,000 resamples). In-distribution results additionally report PR-AUC, macro-F1, MCC and TPR at 1\% FPR.

\emph{Compute and provenance.} The 160 final runs used one code commit and a CPU-only cloud container (4 vCPUs, Intel Xeon at 2.10\,GHz, no GPU); together they took 4.27\,h of wall-clock time (96\,s per run on average). The report scripts refuse to run if code commits differ within an experiment. One run interrupted by a container restart was resumed from the same commit.

\subsection{C1: Class hold-out is often infeasible on time-ordered captures}
\input{tables/feasibility}
Temporal LOACO is infeasible on two of three day-scheduled captures, while natural novelty is available on all three (Table~\ref{tab:feasibility}). In D1 and D3 the time-ordered split puts every attack class wholly in the training period or wholly in the test period (D1: 12 and 2 of 14 classes; D3: 4 and 5 of 9), so no class can be held out of a training pool that contains it. D2 interleaves attacks over time and supports LOACO under both splits but has no test-only classes. \subsection{C3: Natural novelty exposes instability that LOACO hides}

Every supervised score is less stable under natural novelty than under LOACO (Fig.~\ref{fig:seedstd}), by 1.7$\times$ (MSP) to 17$\times$ (kNN). The two seeded benign-only detectors stay below 0.018, below every supervised score's minimum of 0.074; the autoencoder's large ratio comes from a near-zero LOACO baseline and one class (D5 portscan). On D3 natural novelty, kNN has a seed standard deviation of 0.33--0.40 AUC on four of five classes, so the same detector can rank first or last depending on the seed. \textbf{Answer to RQ2 (stability):} one training run says little about a supervised detector's zero-day behaviour, and LOACO understates the variation.

\subsection{C4: Confidence-magnitude scores are invalid in binary NIDS}\label{sec:c4}
\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig_confidence}
\caption{MSP and energy fall below chance wherever the classifier detects unseen attacks confidently, while the attack probability P(attack) and representation distance stay above chance in the mean.}
\label{fig:confidence}
\end{figure}
MSP and energy fall below 0.5 wherever the classifier detects unseen attacks confidently; the attack probability does not (Fig.~\ref{fig:confidence}). On LOACO D2, MSP is below 0.5 on every seed of all eight classes, while P(attack) reaches 0.997--1.000 on the same models: the classifier detects the held-out attacks, and MSP reads that confidence as normality. The same mechanism appears in-distribution, where MSP ranges from 0.075 (D3) to 0.394 (D1) under grouped splits on D1--D3 while P(attack) is 0.812--0.999. P(attack) falls below 0.5 only on individual classes: D3 LOACO ddos (0.35) and scanning (0.46), and D5 natural-novelty portscan (0.16). Representation distance is a valid alternative in the mean, but on natural novelty D3 its interval includes 0.5. \textbf{Answer to RQ3 (validity):} max-softmax and energy should not be used as novelty alarms on binary detectors.

\subsection{C5: Temporal and cross-dataset generalization}
\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig_shift}
\caption{Left: in-distribution AUC under grouped-random vs temporal splits per dataset (solid: AHSD P(attack); dashed: XGBoost). Only on D3 does the neural backbone degrade while XGBoost holds. Right: the 108 zero-shot transfer AUCs (12 detectors $\times$ 9 pairs) cluster around chance.}
\label{fig:shift}
\end{figure}
Temporal-split effects are dataset-specific (Fig.~\ref{fig:shift}, left). On D1 both models lose about 0.27--0.29 AUC; on D2 neither changes by more than 0.014; on D5 both improve; and only on D3 does the neural backbone degrade (0.664, seed std 0.202) while XGBoost holds 0.978. Across the 108 zero-shot transfer results (12 detectors $\times$ 9 source--target pairs; Fig.~\ref{fig:shift}, right) the median AUC is 0.500: 67\% fall in [0.3, 0.6], 12\% below 0.3 and 21\% above 0.6. The highest values come from D1$\rightarrow$D3, where CLAD reaches 0.876 and XGBoost 0.832. \textbf{Answer to RQ3 (shift):} a detector validated on one network, or under one split, must be revalidated before it is trusted elsewhere.


\subsection{Checkpoint sensitivity}
Choosing the final-epoch instead of the best-validation checkpoint moves representation and CLAD family means by at most 0.073 AUC and confidence scores by up to 0.205 on D5, whose train-tail validation set holds only 26 attack windows. Only one benign-only contrast changes sign (natural novelty D1, benign-only minus confidence: $-0.025$ to $+0.032$). The conclusions above therefore do not hinge on checkpoint selection, except for confidence scores where validation data are thin.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:disc}
\subsection{Why the protocols disagree}
LOACO removes one class but keeps the rest of the training distribution, including benign traffic from the hours of the test set. Natural novelty changes the attack class, the benign traffic and often the hosts and tools at once. Supervised representations are shaped by the attacks they were trained to separate: when the shift is small they place a held-out class far from benign traffic (representation 0.977 on D2 LOACO), but when benign traffic itself drifts, as on D3, the same scores fall to chance and become seed-dependent. Benign-only detectors do not depend on which attacks were seen, which explains their stability but not their accuracy: they lead on D3 natural novelty yet trail by 0.17 on D2 LOACO. XGBoost's strength agrees with evidence that tree ensembles remain hard to beat on tabular data~\cite{grinsztajn2022}; flattened flow windows are tabular.

\subsection{Implications for security management}
For an operator, a NIDS is a security service whose assurance depends on evidence gathered before deployment. Our results show that the usual evidence, a single LOACO run, can select the wrong detector family and understate run-to-run variation by up to 17$\times$. Because retraining is routine in operation, a detector whose zero-day coverage depends on the random seed is hard to manage: two retrainings of the same model can catch different new attack classes. We therefore recommend the following checklist for detector assurance:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Evaluate under natural temporal novelty wherever timestamps allow, alongside LOACO, and report when LOACO is infeasible.
\item Do not use max-softmax or energy as novelty alarms on binary detectors; use the attack probability or a representation distance, validated under natural novelty.
\item Include a benign-only detector and a gradient-boosted tree as baselines; one of them matched or beat every dedicated novelty method in five of seven evaluations.
\item Train at least five seeds and report the per-class seed standard deviation, not only the mean.
\item Group flows into time blocks before windowing, deduplicate windows, and purge neighbouring blocks on short captures.
\item Pre-register acceptance criteria and report the ones that fail.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Relation to prior findings}
Our results agree with Zoppi et al. that detectors trained without attack labels can handle unknown attacks well~\cite{zoppi2023}, but only under some protocols. They do not support a general advantage of training with known attacks~\cite{wilkie2026}: CLAD trails benign-only detectors on both D3 evaluations and leads them only on LOACO D2. Reported hold-out results near 0.97~\cite{jodayree2026} are consistent with our LOACO numbers on D2, and our natural-novelty results suggest that they may not hold under temporal shift. Our finding that time-aware evaluation changes conclusions mirrors earlier evidence from malware detection~\cite{pendlebury2019}.

\subsection{Limitations and threats to validity}\label{sec:limits}
\emph{One neural backbone:} all supervised scores come from one recurrent backbone, so Fig.~\ref{fig:shift} is not a claim about neural networks in general. \emph{Small natural-novelty sets:} D1 and D5 contribute two classes each, and the class bootstrap understates their uncertainty; D3 carries most of the evidence. \emph{Thin validation on D5:} 26 attack windows make checkpoint selection fragile for confidence scores. \emph{Design choices:} the 1-hour grouping, any-attack window label, 200-window threshold and 5:1 test ratio were fixed in advance but are not the only reasonable choices. \emph{Post-hoc addition:} P(attack) is labelled and is not confirmatory evidence. \emph{Reimplementation:} CLAD may differ from its authors' code. \emph{Testbed data:} natural novelty follows each capture's schedule, which is closer to deployment than LOACO but is not live traffic.

\section{Conclusion}
How zero-day detection is evaluated decides which detector an operator would deploy. On five public datasets with leakage-controlled splits, 12 detectors and five seeds, class hold-out and natural temporal novelty crowned different winners; natural novelty raised the seed variability of every supervised score by 1.7--17$\times$; max-softmax and energy scores were invalid on binary detectors; and a plain gradient-boosted tree matched or beat the best novelty family in four of seven evaluations. Four pre-registered hypotheses failed their frozen gates, and we report them in full. Future work should extend the comparison to more neural backbones, to datasets with more naturally novel classes and to live traffic.
